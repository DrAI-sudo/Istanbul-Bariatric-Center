When bypass beats sleeve — who should choose Roux-en-Y
A gastric sleeve is a simpler, faster operation with no rerouting. But for some patients, sleeve is the wrong answer. Choose Roux-en-Y gastric bypass if you have any of the following:
	•	Severe gastro-oesophageal reflux (GORD) — a sleeve can make reflux worse; bypass consistently improves it and is the correct primary choice
	•	Large hiatus hernia — bypass allows simultaneous hiatus repair with better long-term reflux control
	•	BMI ≥ 45 kg/m² — long-term weight loss and durability of results are slightly better than sleeve for higher BMIs
	•	Poorly controlled type 2 diabetes — bypass produces faster and more sustained diabetes remission (~74% at 1 year vs ~60% for sleeve (Diabetologia))
	•	Insulin-dependent type 2 diabetes — RYGB is the preferred metabolic operation for this group
	•	Failed gastric band or failed sleeve (revision) — bypass is the standard revisional choice; Dr Üstün has 300+ revisional cases
	•	Barrett’s oesophagus or severe oesophagitis — bypass allows safer long-term management
Not sure which is right for you? Every candidate has a free 20-minute video consultation with Dr Üstün before booking. He will review your BMI, comorbidities, prior operations, endoscopy findings and reflux history, then recommend sleeve, mini bypass or RYGB with the clinical reason for the choice.
How Roux-en-Y gastric bypass works
RYGB is a laparoscopic operation performed under general anaesthesia through 4–5 small keyhole incisions (each 5–12 mm). Operating time is 90–120 minutes. There are four key surgical steps:
Step 1 — Create the pouch
The upper part of the stomach is divided using a linear stapler to create a small pouch of about 30 ml (roughly egg-sized). The rest of the stomach (the remnant) is left in place but food no longer passes through it.
Step 2 — Divide the small bowel
The jejunum is divided approximately 50 cm below the stomach. The distal (downstream) end becomes the Roux limb; the proximal end carries bile and pancreatic juice and becomes the biliopancreatic limb.
Step 3 — Gastrojejunostomy
The Roux limb is brought up and joined to the new stomach pouch. This is the anastomosis food will now pass through, bypassing the remnant stomach, duodenum and proximal jejunum entirely.
Step 4 — Jejunojejunostomy
The biliopancreatic limb is joined to the Roux limb 100–150 cm further down, creating the classic Y-configuration. This is where bile and pancreatic juice re-join the food stream to start digestion. Mesenteric defects are closed to prevent internal hernia.
Why bypass works so well for diabetes: beyond weight loss, the bypass triggers rapid metabolic changes independent of weight — altered gut hormone signalling (GLP-1, PYY), bile acid recirculation, and changes in the gut microbiome. This is why HbA1c and insulin sensitivity improve within days, before significant weight is lost.

Long-term nutrition and lifelong supplementation
Bypass permanently changes how your gut absorbs vitamins and minerals. Missing supplements is the single biggest cause of preventable long-term problems. This protocol is non-negotiable:
Daily (for life)
	•	Bariatric multivitamin (Bariatric Advantage, Celebrate or Fitforme)
	•	Calcium citrate 1,200–1,500 mg (split into 2–3 doses; not with iron)
	•	Vitamin D3 2,000–3,000 IU
	•	Oral iron 45–65 mg elemental (take with vitamin C, away from calcium)
	•	Protein 60–80 g per day from food + shakes
Periodic
	•	Vitamin B12 — intramuscular injection every 3 months (or high-dose oral daily)
	•	Bloods at 3 months, 6 months, 12 months, then annually for life
	•	DEXA scan for bone density every 2–3 years
	•	Annual check: full blood count, iron studies, ferritin, B12, folate, vitamin D, calcium, PTH, HbA1c, lipid profile
Also avoid: NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) — they cause marginal ulcers. Smoking — same reason. Alcohol — absorbed much faster after bypass; drink far less and never on an empty stomach.

What is the difference between gastric bypass and mini gastric bypass?
Roux-en-Y bypass uses two anastomoses in a Y-configuration; mini bypass (OAGB) uses one anastomosis and a longer tubular pouch. Mini bypass is technically simpler and slightly quicker; RYGB has a lower long-term bile-reflux risk and is preferred for revision after failed sleeve. Weight-loss and diabetes-remission outcomes are broadly similar at 5–7 years.
Is gastric bypass reversible?
Technically yes, but reversal is a major operation and rarely done. Treat a bypass as a permanent decision.
Which is better — gastric sleeve or gastric bypass?
Neither is universally better — it depends on your BMI, reflux status and comorbidities. Sleeve is simpler with quicker recovery; bypass gives greater long-term weight loss, better diabetes remission and is the correct choice for severe GORD or hiatus hernia.
How much weight will I lose after gastric bypass?
Average excess weight loss is 65–80% at 18–24 months — roughly 30–35% of your total starting body weight.
Does gastric bypass cure type-2 diabetes?
Bypass produces remission — not permanent cure — in around 74% of type 2 diabetics at 1 year, decreasing to 38–54% at 5 years and beyond. Remission is fastest in patients under 45, with disease duration under 5 years and no insulin use at surgery.
What is dumping syndrome and how common is it?
Dumping happens when sugary or high-fat food passes too quickly from the small pouch into the small intestine. Early dumping (15–30 min after eating) causes nausea, cramps, sweating; late dumping (1–3 hours later) causes hypoglycaemia. Some form of dumping affects 50–70% of RYGB patients in the early months but usually settles by month 6–12.
How long is recovery from gastric bypass?
2 nights in hospital, home in 5–7 days total. Desk work at 10–14 days, physical work at 4 weeks, full gym at 6 weeks. Phased diet takes 6 weeks (liquids → purée → soft solids → full).
What supplements will I need for life?
Bariatric multivitamin daily, calcium citrate 1,200–1,500 mg, vitamin D3 2,000–3,000 IU, oral iron, and 3-monthly vitamin B12 injections. Annual bloods for iron, B12, folate, vitamin D, calcium and PTH. Missing these causes preventable and serious deficiencies.
Can gastric bypass be revised if it fails?
Yes. Options include pouch revision, distalisation, conversion to duodenal switch, or endoscopic TORe (transoral outlet reduction) — non-surgical stitching of the dilated join through the mouth. Dr Üstün has 300+ revisional cases.
